Abstract— Outpatients are rarely told when to leave home, so they either wait in crowded halls or risk missing their turn. We present a prototype that converts a predicted outpatient wait and a live travel-time estimate into a single “leave now” notification. A Random Forest predicts the remaining wait from doctor identity, department, historical average consultation duration, day of week, hour of day, the number of patients ahead in the queue and patient category. The backend compares the predicted wait with the travel time from the patient’s location, obtained from the OpenRouteService API. It notifies the patient when the two approximately converge and revises the notification if the queue drifts from the prediction. Because real hospital records were unavailable, we evaluate the predictor on a 4,000-record synthetic dataset with non-linear, peak-hour queue behaviour. On a held-out 20% split, Random Forest reaches a mean absolute error (MAE) of 4.69 minutes and an R² of 0.965, against 10.64 minutes and 0.810 for Linear Regression. These results describe model behaviour under the simulated process only and do not establish real-world accuracy. The departure alert is presented as a system design and is not evaluated quantitatively. To the best of our knowledge, none of the reviewed studies combined individual wait-time prediction with home-to-hospital travel time to generate proactive departure recommendations.
Index Terms— hospital queue management, wait-time prediction, Random Forest, departure-time notification, travel-time estimation, synthetic data, healthcare optimization.
I. INTRODUCTION
Outpatient departments in busy hospitals are typically organized around loosely estimated wait times rather than firm schedules. A patient who books an appointment, or simply walks in, is seldom given more than a vague sense of how long a consultation queue will take, and is almost never told when to set out for the hospital. This produces two related problems: uncertainty about the length of the wait and, more consequential in daily life, no guidance on when to leave home so as to arrive neither too early nor too late.
The effect is visible in most waiting halls. Patients who leave too early occupy limited seating and corridor space for long stretches. Patients who leave too late risk losing their position in the queue, which creates friction with staff and other patients. Digital appointment platforms allow slot booking and, at best, a coarse queue-position indicator. Departure guidance tied to the patient’s own travel time is absent from these systems.
This paper describes a prototype, the AI-Based Smart Hospital Queue Prediction and Patient Arrival Time Optimization System, that addresses this gap by combining a machine-learning wait-time predictor with a live travel-time estimate. A Random Forest regressor estimates the remaining wait for a given doctor and queue position. A background process compares this shrinking estimate with the patient’s current travel time from the OpenRouteService API and triggers a single “leave now” push notification. If the queue later moves faster or slower than predicted, the system recalculates and issues a correction. Because no real hospital data was available, we evaluate the wait-time predictor on synthetic data and state clearly what such an evaluation can and cannot show.
The contributions of this paper are:
1. Formulation. We frame departure-time guidance as a decision built from two quantities: the predicted remaining wait and the travel time to the hospital.
2. Prototype. We describe a system that issues one “leave now” notification and revises it when the queue drifts from the prediction beyond a set threshold, with staff-side emergency insertion, doctor unavailability and no-show handling.
3. Predictor evaluation. We compare a Random Forest with Linear Regression on a documented synthetic dataset, report MAE, RMSE and R² on a held-out split, and analyse the feature importances of the Random Forest.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III states the research gap. Section IV describes the prototype. Section V describes the data, models and evaluation protocol. Section VI reports results. Section VII discusses limitations. Sections VIII and IX present the conclusion and future work.
II. RELATED WORK
Individual wait-time prediction. A considerable body of work applies supervised learning to individual patient wait or treatment-time prediction. Handayani et al. used a Random Forest model to estimate ophthalmology treatment time from demographic and queue features [1]. Hijry and Olawoyin applied deep neural networks to emergency-room wait-time estimation using a large electronic health record dataset [3]. Ansari et al. proposed summing predicted durations across sequential treatment stages in a mobile application concept [5]. Rastpour and McGregor predicted mental-health-care wait times from highly deidentified records to satisfy privacy constraints [6]. Joseph et al. compared Random Forest and XGBoost, adding weather as an external predictor, for outpatient clinics [9]. Li et al. benchmarked Linear Regression, k-Nearest Neighbours, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting for a children’s hospital, motivated explicitly by helping patients arrive on time [17]. Taton et al. evaluated regression models and an LSTM on simulation-generated queue data [19]. These studies establish Random Forest and related ensembles as common choices for wait-time regression, and they show that simulated data is used where hospital records are restricted.
Aggregate crowding forecasts. A separate strand forecasts department- or hospital-level crowding rather than an individual patient’s wait. Harrou et al. combined a variational autoencoder with several deep architectures to forecast emergency department visit volume [2]. Vural et al. forecast boarding-patient counts to anticipate overcrowding [15] and built an AI framework predicting hourly and daily emergency-department overcrowding for proactive staffing [20]. These approaches do not produce a wait estimate or an alert for a specific patient.
Explainability and location-aware systems. Sulaiman et al. paired machine learning with rule extraction to make emergency length-of-stay predictions interpretable [10]. Moreno-Sánchez et al. applied SHAP-based explainable AI to triage-level admission prediction [11]. Closer to the present work, Teramoto and Kuwata deployed a smartphone guidance application at a large Japanese hospital that uses location information to notify patients as they approach the hospital [7]. It engages only after travel has begun, uses no learned wait-time model and gives no guidance on when to leave home. Eshghali et al. combined a Random Forest surgery-duration model with GIS-based arrival estimation for operating-room scheduling [18], showing that prediction can be paired with location estimation, though for a different problem.
Broader literature. Mi et al. examined AI- and IoT-enabled hospital management for COVID-era makeshift facilities [8]. Kouchaki et al. and Kumar et al. survey AI/IoT healthcare applications and prospective 6G connectivity respectively [12], [14]. Elalouf and Wachtel review practical approaches and research methodologies for emergency-department queueing [4]. Adhicandra et al. apply reinforcement learning with a priority queue for dynamic emergency reprioritisation [13]. Juang et al. use a multimodal deep model to predict unscheduled ED revisits [16].

III. RESEARCH GAP
Prior work on outpatient wait-time prediction [1], [9], [17] estimates how long a patient will wait. The location-aware system reviewed in [7] notifies patients only once they are already close to the hospital. The study that combines prediction with location estimation [18] addresses operating-room scheduling. Aggregate forecasting studies [2], [15], [20] predict department-level crowding. To the best of our knowledge, none of the reviewed studies combined individual wait-time prediction with home-to-hospital travel time to generate proactive departure recommendations.
The gap is therefore not whether wait time can be predicted, since prior work shows it can. It is whether a predicted wait can be converted into a departure instruction for the patient. This paper addresses that gap with a prototype and evaluates the wait-time predictor that drives it, asking whether a Random Forest predicts wait time more accurately than Linear Regression on simulated queue data.
IV. PROPOSED SYSTEM
A. Architecture. The prototype has a patient-facing web application and a staff dashboard, both built with React and Tailwind CSS, communicating with a Python FastAPI backend. PostgreSQL, accessed through SQLAlchemy, stores patients, doctors, appointments and queue records. The Random Forest module runs inside the FastAPI process. Travel time comes from the OpenRouteService API, using the patient’s location obtained through the browser Geolocation API. Alerts are delivered through the Web Push API and Firebase Cloud Messaging. Fig. 1 shows the architecture.
Fig. 1. System architecture.
B. Patient workflow. A patient registers or logs in and selects a symptom category (for example fever, chest pain or stomach pain). This maps to a department through an admin-editable table. The system lists doctors in that department with a live wait-time range. After the patient selects a doctor, the app requests location permission, the Random Forest predicts the wait, and OpenRouteService returns the travel time. Once the two converge, a “leave now” notification is sent. On arrival, the patient checks in by QR code or manual confirmation at reception. Fig. 4 summarises this workflow.
Fig. 4. Patient workflow.
C. Prediction module. Wait time is framed as a regression problem. One Random Forest Regressor is shared across all doctors, with doctor identity as a categorical feature, so new doctors can be added without a new model. The inputs are doctor identity, department, the doctor’s historical average consultation duration, day of week, hour of day, number of patients ahead and patient category (normal or emergency). The prototype reports the prediction as a range and attaches a short reason based on global feature importance. Neither the range nor the reason is evaluated in this study.
D. Departure alert logic. The alert logic follows four steps:
1. Predict the remaining wait for the patient.
2. Obtain the current travel time for the patient’s location from OpenRouteService.
3. Send a “leave now” notification once the remaining predicted wait and the travel time are approximately equal, so that the patient arrives close to their turn.
4. If the logged actual wait later drifts from the original prediction beyond a set threshold, send a corrected notification to any patient who has not yet left.
E. Staff-side operations. An emergency patient can be inserted at the top of a doctor’s queue, which recalculates and re-notifies the affected patients. Automatic emergency detection is intentionally excluded, so the system acts as a prioritisation aid, consistent with [13]. Staff can mark a doctor temporarily unavailable, which pauses new predictions, mark a no-show patient as skipped so the queue continues moving, and edit the symptom-to-department mapping.
V. METHODOLOGY
A. Synthetic dataset. Real hospital wait-time data could not be obtained within the scope of this project because of patient-data privacy restrictions, a constraint also reported in [6]. We therefore generated a synthetic dataset, following the simulation-based approach used in [19]. The generator models six doctors across five departments, each with a distinct average consultation duration. Queue depth is drawn from a Poisson distribution. Day-of-week and hour-of-day multipliers reproduce peak-hour crowding. About seven percent of records are emergency cases with a short wait regardless of queue depth. Gaussian noise is added. For regular patients, wait time is generated multiplicatively from queue length and the day and time effects. The dataset has 4,000 records. This construction makes the wait non-linear in the inputs, which favours models that can represent feature interactions, as discussed in Section VII.
B. Models. Linear Regression is the parametric baseline. It estimates wait time as a weighted linear combination of the one-hot-encoded features and tests whether a more flexible model is needed on this data. The proposed model is a Random Forest Regressor with 200 estimators and a maximum depth of 10, an ensemble of decision trees whose individual outputs are averaged. Both models are trained on identical one-hot-encoded features.
C. Protocol and metrics. The data are split 80/20 into training and test sets, and all results are reported on the held-out test set. We report Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (R²):
MAE = (1/n) Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ| (1)
RMSE = √[(1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²] (2)
R² = 1 − Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² / Σ (yᵢ − ȳ)² (3)
where yᵢ is the actual wait, ŷᵢ the predicted wait, and ȳ the mean actual wait. MAE gives the average absolute deviation in minutes, RMSE penalises large errors more heavily, and R² measures the proportion of variance in actual wait time explained by the model.

VII. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
What the results show. Under the simulated process, a Random Forest predicts wait time more accurately than Linear Regression. Because the data were generated with a multiplicative structure and share their structure with the model inputs, the results show how the models behave under this simulated process. They do not show accuracy on real hospital queues.
Scope of the evaluation. The present study compares Random Forest Regression against a Linear Regression baseline in order to establish the advantages of a non-linear learning approach for outpatient wait-time prediction. Future work will include comparison against additional machine learning baselines such as queue-length heuristics, log-linear models and gradient-boosting methods to further evaluate the relative performance of the proposed approach.
The departure alert is described as a system design and was not evaluated quantitatively, so this study does not measure how much hospital waiting the alert removes or how often it causes a patient to miss a turn.
Asymmetric cost. Arriving early costs the patient time in the hospital. Arriving late can cost the patient their turn. A deployed system should therefore weigh these two errors differently when deciding how close to the predicted consultation time a patient should arrive.
Threats to validity. The data are synthetic and share structure with the model inputs. The doctor’s average consultation duration is a generator parameter, so it is unusually informative. Only one dataset and one train/test split were used. The prediction ranges and the feature-importance reasons offered to patients were not evaluated.
Privacy. The app requests location permission from patients. A real deployment would need explicit consent, a data-retention policy and a fallback for patients who decline location sharing.
Scope of the prototype. Unscheduled walk-in patients are not yet supported, there is no SMS fallback for patients without reliable internet access, emergency insertion is staff-assisted rather than automatic, and the system targets a single hospital rather than a multi-hospital network.
VIII. CONCLUSION
We presented a prototype that combines Random Forest wait-time prediction with a travel-time check to issue a single “leave now” notification, and we evaluated the predictor on a 4,000-record synthetic dataset. Random Forest reduced MAE from 10.64 to 4.69 minutes and raised R² from 0.810 to 0.965 relative to Linear Regression. These findings describe behaviour under a simulated, multiplicative process and do not establish real-world accuracy. The main contribution is the combination of an individual wait-time prediction with home-to-hospital travel time into a departure instruction, a combination that, to the best of our knowledge, was not present in the reviewed studies. The proposed framework demonstrates the feasibility of integrating wait-time prediction and travel-time awareness within a unified outpatient queue management system.
IX. FUTURE WORK
Future work includes a pilot at a partner hospital, subject to data-sharing and ethics approval, to retrain and validate the model on real queue records. It also includes comparison against stronger baselines (a queue-length heuristic, a log-linear model and gradient boosting) and a simulation of departure policies that measures hospital waiting time and missed turns. Other extensions are support for unscheduled walk-in patients, an SMS fallback channel, multi-hospital deployment with a configurable symptom-to-department mapping, and a retraining pipeline that uses logged predicted-versus-actual waits.
